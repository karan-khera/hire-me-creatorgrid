Case studyGoogle Play Gift Cards

90% played mobile games.
Only 5% bought our gift cards.

How I diagnosed a growth constraint in FamPay's biggest revenue category, corrected the product's discovery journey and built more efficient ways to acquire and retain buyers.

FamPay (now FamApp)Consumer fintech2022–2023Product & GrowthMy RoleRevenue Growth, Product & Monetisation
The Story in Brief

FamPay (now FamApp) is a payments app for young Indians. Google Play Recharge Codes were among its largest revenue categories, allowing users to fund games, apps and in-app purchases without a bank card. Yet only 5% of monthly transacting users purchased them.

I led the diagnosis and cross-functional response with Product, Design and Marketing: identify the real customer use case, repair discovery and allocate incentives according to behaviour. Buyer adoption ultimately rose to 18%, category revenue increased 47%, and earn-to-burn efficiency improved approximately 2×.

01Diagnose the Gap

The numbers suggested demand. The interviews revealed where it went.

Earlier surveys showed that nine in ten users played mobile games. But gaming activity did not automatically translate into demand for paid in-game items. I separated existing buyers from non-buyers and spent two weeks combining transaction analysis, surveys, funnel data and focused group discussions.

With buyers, I followed the transaction patterns. A small set of denominations, including ₹40, ₹80, ₹160 and ₹400, appeared disproportionately often. The values matched purchases within BGMI and Free Fire. Spikes in order volume lined up with in-game events, something users in our discussion groups helped explain.

With non-buyers, I examined awareness, intent and funnel drop-offs. Roughly two-thirds didn't know FamPay sold Google Play codes. Some were already buying them elsewhere, often because competitors offered better deals or felt easier to use. The research also narrowed the real opportunity: approximately 40% of users played regularly and had considered buying codes, while the near-term obtainable market was estimated at around 20% of monthly transacting users.

Three Findings That Changed the Plan
1

Awareness was a measurable constraint.

Most non-buyers hadn't entered the funnel at all. Implication: make the product discoverable to people with a genuine gaming use case, instead of assuming its placement was enough.

2

Demand followed games and events.

Purchase denominations and order spikes reflected what users could buy inside BGMI and Free Fire. Implication: align the product message and campaign calendar to actual gaming behaviour.

3

Discounts weren't equally valuable to everyone.

Some customers were already frequent buyers; others needed a stronger first-purchase reason. Implication: target incentives to the behaviour we wanted to change.

The Growth Target

Move adoption from 5% toward 20% of monthly transacting users, without making every new purchase dependent on a discount.

02Fix Discovery

We were promoting games our users didn't play.

The product surfaces featured Temple Run and Asphalt 8 as examples of where Google Play codes could be used. Our research pointed overwhelmingly to BGMI and Free Fire. This was a positioning problem visible within the app itself.

I mapped the discovery touchpoints with Design and Marketing, then helped run experiments across three surfaces: game-specific creative in the purchase journey, a more prominent home-screen entry point for relevant users, and an updated Google Play Store listing. We iterated placements and copy through A/B tests rather than rolling out one large redesign at once.

03Target Incentives

Incentives worked better when they matched buying behaviour.

The original approach broadly offered buyers 4× FamCoins, with periodic cashback or discounts layered on top. Transaction analysis showed different users needed different interventions. I used recency, frequency and spend to redesign how we allocated the promotional budget.

One revenue category. Three distinct growth jobs.
CohortBehaviourStrategy
Pro BuyersAlready purchased frequentlyReduce blanket incentives; use relevant reminders and protect margins.
Enthu BuyersPurchased occasionally, often around game eventsTime nudges and limited offers to high-intent gaming moments.
High-Potential Non-BuyersEngaged users with interest or purchase-funnel signalsTest targeted first-purchase offers rather than rewarding everyone.
Commercial Outcome

Earn-to-burn efficiency improved approximately 2× as the team shifted from blanket incentives to more selective spend.

This mattered because a higher transaction count was only useful if the category remained economically attractive. We were improving the quality of growth, not simply buying more volume.

04Build Repeat Engagement

We connected the growth plan to the rhythm of the games.

The research showed that game updates, limited-time offers and in-game events moved purchasing behaviour. I worked with Marketing, Performance Marketing and product teams to turn those signals into a more repeatable operating cadence.

Event Signals

Track sudden changes in code purchases, gaming-community discussions and scheduled game events so campaigns could respond when users were most likely to buy.

Lifecycle Nudges

Use timely in-app messages and notifications, while testing the point at which more communication became counterproductive.

Community & Social

Support gaming-related giveaways, tournaments and challenges with the Marketing team; bring high-frequency users into feedback and event communities.

Commercial Leverage

Use growing transaction volume to negotiate better margins with our gift-card supplier, alongside more disciplined incentive spending.

The buyer share stabilised at around 18%, close to the original 20% ambition. The category also became more efficient to grow, giving us a stronger commercial result than simply increasing promotional spend.

—Outro

The growth came from diagnosis, discipline and iteration.

What I'd repeat: Begin with buyer and non-buyer behaviour, not a preferred feature or campaign. Put the actual user journey beside transaction data. Test copy and placement before investing heavily in engineering. Match incentives to the specific behaviour they are meant to change.

What I'd change: We invested engineering effort in tiered rewards that underperformed, and some campaigns pushed notifications hard enough for users to switch them off. I'd run smaller behavioural tests before committing development resources, set communication-frequency guardrails earlier and define one measurable objective per experiment.

The structural risk: Demand still depended on external gaming platforms. Restrictions on a major game could disrupt the category quickly. I'd build a broader set of use cases and contingency plans alongside the growth engine.

The work showed how user evidence, product decisions, marketing operations and commercial discipline can reinforce one another, even when the growth problem initially looks like a demand problem.
