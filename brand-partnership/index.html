Case studyFamPay Rewards

We built an arcade.
Then came 150+ brand campaigns a month.

How I helped turn FamPay Rewards into a repeatable growth channel for 100+ brand partners, while making offers more relevant to users and campaigns easier to operate.

FamPay (now FamApp)Consumer fintech2022–2023Product & GrowthMy RoleMonetisation, Product & Operations
The Story in Brief

FamPay (now FamApp) is a payments app for young Indians, with more than one million monthly transacting users at the time. Within the app, FamPay Rewards was a dedicated destination for brand deals, offers and benefits, alongside games and FamCoin-powered giveaways. It brought a substantial daily audience to brands seeking to reach younger consumers.

My role was to connect that audience with relevant brand offers while building the commercial and operational capacity to deliver them at scale. We redesigned discovery and campaign operations, eventually supporting 150+ live campaigns a month across 100+ brand partners and growing the channel to approximately $1.4M in annualised partnership revenue.

01Find the Constraint

The audience was there. The brands were there. Matching them was the challenge.

The opportunity had two sides. Rewards was already a high-traffic destination, while brand partners wanted meaningful attention from those users. But showing the same catalogue of offers to everyone was neither relevant to users nor efficient for advertisers.

The problem: How could we connect the right brand with the right user without adding friction for users or creating a custom operation for every campaign?

My commercial responsibility was to grow partnership revenue while protecting engagement. We first tested how far the existing experience could scale: a setup comfortable with roughly 20 campaigns began exposing discovery friction around 50–60 offers and became operationally strained as campaign volume approached 120.

The diagnosis was specific. Discovery: users saw too many irrelevant offers. Production: every new campaign demanded repetitive design and configuration work. Ownership: one brand could have different internal owners across Rewards properties.

We also learned that engagement had a cost. In one comparison, an iPhone Spinner generated 2.3× the participation of a Raffle, but also 2.29× the net FamCoin burn. More activity wasn't automatically better unit economics.

02Design for Relevance

The right offers mattered more than showing every offer.

I used transaction-category affinity and RFM signals (recency, frequency and monetary value) to shape overlapping interest cohorts. We refreshed membership roughly every four weeks, suppressed irrelevant categories and retained room for brand commitments and controlled experiments.

This was behaviour-led merchandising, not an automated card-ranking algorithm. The team still ordered individual offers within categories using performance, relevance and commercial priorities.

Segmentation also forced us to distinguish assumed interests from actual redemption behaviour. Food-delivery offers seemed promising based on transaction data, but users were reluctant to leave a food app to hunt for a coupon. Packaged-food campaigns performed better.

We used those learnings to refine offer copy, creative treatments, urgency and WhatsApp reactivation. The objective was a repeatable testing discipline that partners could benefit from, rather than one-off optimisations for every launch.

03Scale the Operation

The product experience improved. The operating system had to catch up.

Campaign volume exposed a second problem: each new brand activation consumed too much manual work, and accountability was split across Rewards properties. I worked with Product, Engineering and Partnerships to make launches repeatable.

1

Build Reusable Campaign Tooling

I scoped improvements to the internal product-ops tool: duplicate an existing campaign, edit copy and dates directly, and support richer creative formats. This removed repeated Figma exports and uploads from routine changes.

2

Give Each Brand One Accountable Owner

We shifted from organising Product Ops by Rewards property to assigning one owner per brand. A partner running raffles, games and offers now had a clearer handoff and a single point of accountability.

3

Operate on a Shared Cadence

Weekly partnership handovers, campaign plans, performance tracking and reviews aligned the four-person Product Ops team with commercial and creative teams. We also improved impression measurement so partners were assessed on actual exposure, not page visits alone.

The improvement mattered because the team could spend more time on campaign quality and partner outcomes, instead of rebuilding the same assets and fields repeatedly. Scaling brands required a system that scaled with them.

04Prove It with a Brand

ITC Classmate showed what a repeatable partnership could look like.

ITC Classmate is one of India's leading notebook and school-stationery brands. Part of ITC, a major Indian consumer-goods company, it sells notebooks, pens and other products familiar to generations of students. Classmate had launched photo-customised notebooks for high-school students, allowing students to personalise their notebook covers.

I helped translate the launch brief into a coordinated eight-week brand programme across FamPay's Rewards properties, games, in-app offers and social media, rather than a one-off placement. Our earlier experiments had given us confidence in how different formats drove discovery and conversion, so we pitched a sequence of campaigns with distinct roles across the customer journey.

The Partnership in Four Parts
Situation

A newly launched personalised notebook needed awareness and purchase among high-school students.

Task

Turn a product launch brief into a repeatable brand programme, rather than one sponsored placement.

Action

Pitch and execute eight weeks of Rewards games, in-app offers and coordinated social promotion.

Result

Stronger return and daily sales helped extend the relationship to about six months.

The programme moved from curiosity through notebook raffles, to discovery through Spin & Win and social campaigns, and finally to purchase through time-bound cashback and FamCard-linked offers. This gave Classmate more than a single moment of exposure and gave us opportunities to learn and optimise across the series.

The campaign also reached people beyond FamPay's owned channels. Independent YouTube creators explained the offers, and deal publishers later documented FamPay–Classmate promotions. That third-party attention showed the offer had relevance beyond our in-app communications.

—Outro

What scaled, and what I'd do differently.

Across the branded-partnership business, we grew to 100+ brand partners and 150+ live campaigns per month. Rewards-page conversion on brand offers improved 3×, while partnership revenue grew 10× to approximately $1.4M annualised. We achieved that scale without adding to our four-person Product Ops team, using better tooling, clear ownership and operating routines while keeping a disciplined partnership P&L. The gains came from linking user relevance, brand performance and operating capacity, rather than spending indiscriminately to buy growth.

What I'd repeat: Start lean. Test demand against real user behaviour before committing time and money to a redesign, a new campaign format or a larger team. Give each brand one accountable owner and build reusable systems around what the tests actually validate.

What I'd change: Give every experiment one primary objective. Reach, conversion and revenue can pull a campaign in different directions; I'd separate them into focused two- to three-week windows, for example testing impressions first and conversion next, rather than asking one experiment to optimise everything. I'd also introduce tighter guardrails around offer density and promotional claims earlier, so engagement never comes at the expense of user trust.

The lesson: brand partnerships scale when relevant offers work for users, measurable performance works for brands, and the operating system lets a lean team deliver profitably.
