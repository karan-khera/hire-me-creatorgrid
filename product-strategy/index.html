Case studyMonzy

Helping people pick a credit card, instead of being sold one.

How I tested the premise behind a new credit-card discovery business, recommended a practical first product, and built the data and decision logic to support it.

MonzyEarly-stage personal financeJune–November 2025Product discovery engagementMy roleProduct strategy consultant
The story in brief

Our hypothesis was that credit-card discovery could start with a person's needs instead of issuer promotion. I validated the opportunity, built the case for a questionnaire-led MVP, and developed the 500+ card database and initial decision logic. Over six months, we also established a live content presence and progressed commercial infrastructure discussions. Together, these created a concrete foundation for the first product.

01Frame the opportunity

The starting question was about trust, not card inventory.

Credit-card discovery in India is often shaped by sales incentives: a bank representative promotes a product, a comparison site ranks popular offers, or someone takes a friend's recommendation. None necessarily begins with how the person will use the card.

Our hypothesis was that discovery could work in reverse. Start with eligibility, spending habits and preferences; then identify relevant options and explain the trade-offs. The project would sit within Ankur Warikoo's existing company. Ankur is one of India's leading finance creators, but the long-term ambition was to build a standalone business with an independent brand.

My mandate had three parts: identify and validate the gap, assess whether it could support a viable business, and determine what we could credibly build first. That meant customer and competitive research, product scoping, data design and early commercial feasibility.

02Find the evidence

People didn't need another list of cards. They needed help deciding.

I combined user interviews with competitive research to understand where existing card-discovery journeys fell short and what Monzy could do differently.

Three findings that changed the product brief
1

Recommendations often ignored individual fit.

People acquired cards through familiar or convenient channels, then discovered that benefits didn't match their spending. Implication: ask about eligibility, real usage and priorities before generating a shortlist.

2

Trust depended on explaining the recommendation.

Opaque rankings and sales-led advice made it hard to distinguish suitability from commission. Implication: make card attributes comparable and explain the reasons behind a recommendation.

3

Research was fragmented across too many sources.

Users assembled information from issuers, peers and online communities. Implication: build a reliable common card dataset and publish useful content that earns attention independently.

03Choose the MVP

The best data source wasn't the best place to start.

Personalisation needed information about spending and preferences. I evaluated four ways to capture it, comparing the value of each method with the effort, permissions and friction it introduced.

Our initial team ranking put Account Aggregator first. It offered richer financial signals but required regulated access, integration and consent flows—dependencies that could delay an initial launch by six months or more.

My recommendation

Launch with a short, high-signal questionnaire. Learn whether users value the recommendations before investing in a complex financial-data integration.

We aligned on the questionnaire-first route while leaving richer transaction data as a potential later capability. We would still personalise. We chose a version we could test in weeks rather than months.

04Build the foundation

A simple questionnaire still needs a serious engine behind it.

The questionnaire was only the front end. Behind it, I had to make thousands of card attributes consistent, decide which ones mattered to users, and establish the quality checks needed before engineering could build on the data.

1

Build a comparable card database.

I structured 500+ credit cards with roughly 50–60 data points each, creating a working base of approximately 30,000 card-level data points. Fees, eligibility, reward rules, networks, benefits and exclusions were standardised so cards could be compared on the same terms.

2

Design the decision and interface hierarchy.

I prioritised the fields that mattered most to a user's decision: eligibility, spending use case, fees, reward value and restrictions. I mapped them into questionnaire flows, filters and an initial rules-based decision tree, so the interface could explain why a card might fit rather than display a long list of attributes.

3

Establish a QA/QC gate for engineering.

I defined a review process to check field completeness, reconcile values with issuer terms, test consistency between similar cards and flag ambiguous benefit conditions. Decision-critical fields and unresolved exceptions were tracked for review before engineering sign-off. The aim was a dependable data specification and clear recommendation logic, not just a populated spreadsheet.

We tested sample questionnaire responses against the database manually to examine how the filters and rules behaved. The output was a structured information base, an initial decision model and a documented approach to checking data quality before implementation.

05Prepare to reach users

A recommendation product also needed distribution and partners.

Distribution had two distinct workstreams: SEO content designed to earn search traffic, and YouTube experiments designed to learn what audiences responded to. I helped set content priorities and owned the accuracy of credit-card information published through both channels.

SEO: identify content that could earn organic discovery.

We launched a landing page and an SEO-led blog ahead of the recommendation experience. I helped identify high-intent questions, comparisons and card use cases worth covering, and validated the figures, issuer terms and benefit explanations in published content. The SEO lead owned article creation, technical optimisation and publishing execution.

YouTube: experiment with topics and audience response.

We tested independent credit-card videos to see which subjects resonated without promotion from the founder's audience. The YouTube lead owned video concepts, hooks, scripts and production; I reviewed the accuracy of card claims, reward calculations and use-case examples before content went out.

Commercial feasibility: evaluate potential partners.

I led discussions and analysis with issuers, aggregators and infrastructure providers, covering API access, integration feasibility and potential referral economics. This helped define how the product could connect recommendations to a sustainable commercial model.

The commercial guardrail

Recommend the best-fit card even when the platform earns no commission.

—Outro

What we built, and what I learned.

During my stint, I helped move Monzy from our initial hypothesis to a concrete product direction: evidence of user needs, a viable first journey, a structured 500+ card knowledge base, preliminary recommendation rules, live educational content and progress on commercial infrastructure.

The next build priorities: turn the manual recommendation tests into an automated matching journey, and connect users to relevant application flows.

Early organic traction: In the first two weeks following launch, around 5,000 people went through Monzy’s discovery funnel through organic channels, without promotion by Ankur or other creators. Around one in five visitors came through AI-assisted discovery, including search and assistant citations. These were encouraging early signals for the SEO content framework we had put in place.

What I'd change: my initial decision tree was too rigid for overlapping user preferences. When that assumption was challenged, it became clear that a more flexible preference and ranking model would better handle competing user needs.

The lesson I took away: in early-stage work, the fastest useful test usually beats the best data source.
